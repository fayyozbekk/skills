Concise writing is not measured in words on the page — it is measured in how long it takes a reader to process them. Readers care about *who* is doing *what*; that is where their attention goes first. Put the character your reader cares about in the subject slot, and put the action that matters in the verb slot. Everything else here follows from that.

## When invoked

**Read** the whole passage first — lock in voice, register, and intent before editing.
**Find** the slow points: open cores, nominalizations, missing connectors.
**Rewrite** those parts and return the revision. Explain a change only if the user asks.

## What to preserve

- **Voice and register.** Academic stays academic, casual stays casual. Match the writer.
- **Technical terms.** Domain words are usually load-bearing — don't swap them for plain synonyms.
- **Sentences that already work.** Revise the slow points; leave the fast ones alone.

## The Formula

Build long sentences as:

**(CO + CORE + other)ⁿ**

| Slot | What it contains |
|------|------------------|
| **CO** | **C**onnectors and **O**rientors. Short cues placed before the core (*because*, *although*, *During 2020*, *According to Keynes*). |
| **CORE** | The subject and verb of the clause, kept close together so the reader can locate them quickly. |
| **other** | Material that follows the verb — its object plus modifiers, examples, or elaborations. |
| **n** | How many times the unit repeats. A long sentence is several short, well-formed clauses chained together. |

A clean example mapped onto the formula:

> *This surprising recovery* **continued** *into the fourth quarter*, **even though** *manufacturers of consumer durables* **reduced** *inventories in a near panic*, **because** *retail sales* **had slowed** *abruptly*.

| CO | CORE (subject + verb) | other |
|----|----------------------|-------|
| — | This surprising recovery continued | into the fourth quarter |
| even though | manufacturers of consumer durables reduced | inventories in a near panic |
| because | retail sales had slowed | abruptly |

The sentence is long. It does not feel long, because the reader meets a fresh subject–verb pair every few words and is told in advance — by *even though*, *because* — how each new pair relates to the last.

## CORE — subject and verb

Every English sentence has a subject and a verb. You cannot avoid having a core, but you control two things: what fills those slots, and how close together they sit.

### Readers search for the verb

Until the reader finds the verb, they are holding the subject in mind without knowing what is being said about it. A long gap between subject and verb forces them to carry a growing load before any meaning resolves — a *core violation*.

> ✗ **Open core:** *This surprising recovery in the face of an abrupt slowing of retail sales in the consumer durable goods sector with the consequent near panic of inventory reductions among manufacturers* **continued** into the fourth quarter.

> ✓ **Closed core:** *This surprising recovery* **continued** into the fourth quarter…

Same information. In the second, the verb sits next to its subject, so the reader knows what is happening within six words.

**Diagnostic:** underline every word before the first verb. If that span runs three lines, the core is open — close it.

### Characters go in the subject; actions go in the verb

Every sentence tells a story. Readers expect to see *who* (a character) doing *what* (an action), and they grasp meaning fastest when the subject of your sentence *is* the main character and the verb *is* the main action. When those don't line up, prose feels indirect, abstract, and dense; readers have to fill in missing pieces from their own knowledge — often guessing wrong.

A character is something that can act or be acted on, *as the reader perceives it*, not as you do. The character is usually concrete (a person, a team, a thing). Abstractions can serve as characters, but only if the reader already perceives them as doers.

**Pull the character out of the nouns.** When the action is buried in a noun, the character usually goes missing too:

> ✗ Our loss of funding prevented continuation of the research program.

> ✓ Because we lost funding, we could not continue the research program.

The first hides the character (*we*) inside *loss* and *continuation*. The second puts *we* in the subject slot and gives it real verbs.

**Pick the concrete character over the abstract one.** When several candidates compete for the subject slot, prefer the one the reader can picture:

> ✗ Shifts in position in recent decades in three bands of fast-moving wind known as jet streams came from a new analysis of weather data…

> ✓ Three bands of fast-moving wind — known as jet streams — have shifted position in recent decades, according to a new analysis of weather data…

Both sentences are grammatical. The second is faster because *jet streams* (concrete) sits where *shifts* (abstract) used to.

**A four-step diagnosis** for a foggy sentence:

1. Skim the first seven or eight words.
2. Identify the main characters — who or what the sentence is really about.
3. Locate the actions those characters perform.
4. Rebuild so each character is a subject and each action is a verb, joined by connectors like *because*, *although*, *if*, *when*, *how*, *why*.

### Active vs. passive — choose by character, not by rule

There is usually more than one way to close a core:

- **Active:** *Using a standard data collection form, our team will obtain information regarding…*
- **Passive:** *Using a standard data collection form, information will be obtained regarding…*

Both close the core. The passive is not wrong; it is a legitimate tool for putting the right character in the subject slot. Pick whichever puts the character the reader cares about there, and gets to the verb quickly.

### Nominalizations hide the action

A nominalization is a verb (or adjective) turned into a noun. They often end in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ence*, *-ance*, *-ity*.

| Verb | Nominalization | Adjective | Nominalization |
|------|----------------|-----------|----------------|
| discover | discovery | careless | carelessness |
| resist | resistance | different | difference |
| react | reaction | proficient | proficiency |

Nominalizations work against the formula because they hide both the action and the actor:

> ✗ Our request is that you review the data.
> ✓ We request that you review the data.

> ✗ There is disagreement among many experts about the utility of emissions cap-and-trade policies.
> ✓ Experts disagree about whether emissions cap-and-trade policies are useful.

A subtler case shows what nominalization lets writers skip past entirely:

> This letter provides our discussion of the introduction of poultry cold cuts into Acme's Excelsior line.

The sentence reads cleanly. But try to denominalize *discussion* and *introduction* into verbs and three pieces of information turn out to be missing:

- **Character.** Who discusses? Who introduces? The same agent (Acme describing its own plans) or two (an outside consultant analyzing Acme)?
- **Logic.** Two verbs need a connector between them — *whether*, *why*, *how*, *when* — and each yields a different sentence.
- **Time.** Real verbs need real tense, forcing you to commit to whether the introduction has happened, will happen, or is being weighed.

When you find a nominalization in a passage that is supposed to carry an argument, ask whether converting it back to a verb would let you name a character, add a connector, and pin down a tense.

## CO — Connectors and Orientors

CO covers the short, lightweight phrases that appear before the core and tell the reader how to interpret what is coming. Two distinct kinds, doing different jobs.

### Orientors — short context cues

An orientor supplies background — typically place, time, source, or purpose — before the core arrives.

| Type | Example |
|------|---------|
| Place | **In both London and Geneva,** we have well-established positions in underwriting Euro-securities. |
| Time | **During 2020,** we sold our coatings operations in Europe and Mexico. |
| Source | **According to Keynes,** there are many reasons businessmen may not choose to invest. |
| Purpose | **To cope with this trend,** we have emphasized the need for resource planning. |

Orientors earn their place by being short. An orientor that runs several lines (a tax-code phrase like "For purposes of determining the amount of its gross income…") becomes its own core violation. Treat it as another core to close.

### Connectors — logic words that carry the argument

Connectors signal the relationship between cores. They do not just glue clauses together; they tell the reader what move you are making.

| Function | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| Linking | and, or, nor, moreover, furthermore, likewise, in addition, similarly, that, which |
| Contrast | but, yet, however, nevertheless, although, on the contrary, on the other hand |
| Time / place | until, when, where |
| Causal | because, since, why, therefore, consequently, so, thus, hence |
| Means | how, by |
| Condition | if/then, when/then, whenever, unless, whether |
| Purpose | so that, in order to |

Connectors are not optional decoration — they carry the argument. Take three bare facts:

> Twinkies were being served at the party. I left at 7pm. Chad was there.

Without connectors, the reader guesses at the relationship. Add them, and the meaning becomes specific *and reversible*:

> **Although** Twinkies were being served at the party, I left at 7pm **because** Chad was there.

> **Since** Twinkies were being served at the party, I left at 7pm **even though** Chad was there.

Same three facts, two completely different stories. When an editor tells you to "cut unnecessary words," do not assume connectors are unnecessary. In speech, tone and gesture supply the logic; in writing, only the connectors do.

### A note on *because*

Some writers — especially in the sciences — avoid *because* out of fear that it asserts causality from too few data points. There are two distinct uses:

- **Causal in the world:** *The vase broke because the cat knocked it off the windowsill.* Claims a cause-and-effect relation in reality.
- **Justificatory in the text:** *We can infer that the vase must have been broken, because a hairline crack runs up its front.* Signals that one part of the text supplies grounds for a claim in another part.

The justificatory sense is exactly what scholarly readers want to see. Withholding *because* in that role doesn't make the writing more cautious; it just makes the logic harder to find.

## other — what follows the verb

The third slot covers everything after the verb: object, modifiers, examples, lists, qualifications. This is where most of the sentence's detail typically lives.

Once the core is closed, the reader has a stable platform on which to receive new information. Detail that would have been overwhelming before the verb becomes manageable after it, because the reader already knows who is doing what. The *other* slot is where a long sentence earns its length — long lists and qualifications can pile up here without slowing the reader, provided each clause's core is already in place.

**Prefer connectors over strings of prepositions.** Prepositional chains link nouns to nouns and tend to produce nominalized, low-meaning text:

> ✗ …slowing of retail sales in the consumer durable goods sector with the consequent near panic of inventory reductions among manufacturers.

Connectors link verbs to verbs and carry far more meaning per word:

> ✓ …reduced inventories in a near panic because retail sales had slowed abruptly.

When the relationship between ideas matters, reach for a connector, not a preposition.

## n — chaining

The exponent is what makes this a formula for *long* sentences. Once you can build one (CO + CORE + other) unit cleanly, you can chain several together and the sentence stays readable. The recovery example chains three units across thirty-some words and reads easily — because each unit is short, and each connector tells the reader in advance how the next unit relates to the one before.

The reframing: stop measuring sentences by word count, and start measuring them by *time* — by how long it takes a reader to find the verb. A short sentence with a buried verb is harder than a long sentence with three closed cores.

## Checklist for revision

- **Find the verb in each long sentence.** Underline every word before it. If the span runs three lines, the core is open — close it.
- **Close the core by choosing your character.** Promote a character to the subject (often active voice) or move the existing subject's verb up next to it (often passive voice). Pick whichever puts the character the reader cares about in the subject slot.
- **Skim for connectors.** If you are arguing but see almost no *because*, *although*, *if/then*, *even though*, your logic is probably trapped inside nominalizations. Convert the nominalizations back to verbs and the connectors will appear on their own.
- **Check your orientors.** They earn their place by being short. If an opening phrase has grown to several lines, treat it as another core to close.
- **Long is not the problem; an open core is.** A sentence built as (CO + CORE + other)ⁿ can be long and still feel short.
